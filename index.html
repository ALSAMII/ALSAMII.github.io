    Door

      Nothing matches that.

        Read the story — PDF

        For years I read other people’s stories and lived in worlds someone else had already decided the shape of. Then a drug took the walls off one night and I made my own — planner, participant, whole production crew — and I have not been a guest since, so I finish them here.

        Short noir novellas about people who finally say it out loud. Every book works the same way — a door out of the mind, a room on the other side, and the key that opened it.

        The Delgoshāدلگشا

        Manuchehr Delgoshā is the most famous joke writer in the country, and no one has ever heard his name — for thirty-eight years, every line he’s written has been spoken by someone else, in rooms he was never in. He built it that way at nineteen, on the conviction that a sentence with no sender cannot be prosecuted. In all that time, he has never once laughed at his own work. Then Roshanak Azimi hires him to write a speech she cannot write herself, delivers it, and afterwards says six words that make him laugh for the first time in eighteen years. Together they build Romanu, a brand with no name behind it — and when a machine is trained to write jokes just like his, Manu answers by finally publishing the ones he always refused to give away.         The Unsaidناگفته

        THE UNSAID is named for nāgofteh — the silence left in a room after the state has gone home. Each book writes whoever stood closest to it: a mother whose dreams of her dead son are recorded each morning and handed back to her in translation, reworded into something she no longer recognizes as grief; a crane operator who loses his sense of weight entirely; a judge who never hears the hours someone is counting on the other side of the city. None of them is a monster. Each one simply trusted that someone further down the line would be the one to feel it. Start anywhere: each book opens with enough of a recap to stand on its own.

        The Unheard House

        The House is a walled colony outside Tabriz where the illness takes the nerve, and nobody inside can be trusted to feel what is happening to them. Three novellas — a cutting room thirty-four years late, the wall itself in 1938, and a company in Rotterdam that has worked out what the place was for — about what two hundred people gave their hands and their eyes to protect.

        Author's Notes

        Some thoughts can't be said out loud without changing how the room sees you afterward. So they don't get said.

        Not shameful thoughts. The partly true ones. Everyone keeps one, unsaid, for years—and unsaid is what keeps it alive. Sooner or later it wants a body that isn't yours. I give it one.

        The mind negotiates. Given a minute alone it will reframe anything, delay anything, forgive itself in advance. The body won't. It keeps the actual record, underneath the words, where no conversation reaches—which is why nothing here gets settled by one. An ordeal is the only interrogation that works on a witness who doesn't talk.

        Nothing here resolves. I'm suspicious of anyone whose does.

        Call it noir, transgressive, plausible—most of these are all three. Something bad happens to someone who's earned about half of it, and nobody gets saved. I don't cut away from the ugly part. And it's plausible: everything in these books could actually happen. I let myself bend one thing about the world per book—never two—and everything else stays exactly as ordinary as it already is.

        Bring your ugliest thought. There's a chair for it.

        — Chew Z
